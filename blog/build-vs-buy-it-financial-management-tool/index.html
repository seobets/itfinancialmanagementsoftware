Should You Build or Buy an IT Financial Management (ITFM) Tool?

IT spending is becoming increasingly complex. Organizations must manage cloud consumption, software licensing, infrastructure costs, shared services, workforce expenses, and technology investments while demonstrating how IT spending supports business outcomes.

For many organizations, spreadsheets and disconnected reporting tools are no longer sufficient. IT leaders need a reliable way to understand technology costs, allocate expenses to business units, forecast budgets, and communicate the business value of IT investments.

This creates an important strategic question: Should you build or buy an IT Financial Management (ITFM) tool?

Building a custom solution offers flexibility and control, while buying a commercial platform provides established capabilities, structured workflows, and vendor support. A hybrid approach can also combine a commercial ITFM foundation with custom integrations and analytics.

The right choice depends on your organization's financial management maturity, technical capabilities, data architecture, budget, and long-term objectives.

In this guide, we explore the advantages and disadvantages of building versus buying an ITFM tool, compare the total cost of ownership, and explain how to choose the approach that best fits your business.

What Is IT Financial Management (ITFM)?

IT Financial Management is the practice of planning, tracking, analyzing, allocating, and optimizing the costs associated with delivering IT services and capabilities.

ITFM helps organizations connect technology spending with business services, operational requirements, and strategic priorities.

An effective ITFM capability typically supports:

  • IT cost transparency: Understanding where technology spending occurs and what drives it.
  • Cost allocation: Distributing technology costs across business units, products, services, and consumers.
  • Budgeting and forecasting: Planning future expenditure and identifying budget variances.
  • Showback and chargeback: Communicating IT consumption costs to business stakeholders or allocating those costs to their budgets.
  • Technology investment planning: Evaluating the financial implications of technology decisions.
  • Cost optimization: Identifying inefficiencies, duplication, unused resources, and opportunities to reduce spending.
  • Executive reporting: Translating technical expenditure into meaningful financial and business insights.

ITFM often works alongside Technology Business Management (TBM), which provides a structured approach to connecting technology costs with the services and business outcomes they support.

For organizations with significant public-cloud expenditure, ITFM may also overlap with FinOps, which focuses on the financial accountability and optimization of cloud usage.

Although these disciplines are related, they are not interchangeable. A cloud cost management tool may not provide the broader allocation, budgeting, and service-cost modeling capabilities required for enterprise ITFM.

Why Are Organizations Evaluating ITFM Tools?

The decision to build or buy usually emerges when existing financial management processes become difficult to scale.

Several common challenges drive this evaluation.

1. Fragmented IT financial data

Technology costs may be distributed across ERP systems, general ledgers, cloud billing platforms, procurement applications, asset management tools, and spreadsheets.

Combining this information into a consistent financial view can require significant manual effort.

2. Limited cost transparency

Organizations may know how much they spend on IT without understanding the cost of individual services, products, applications, or business capabilities.

Without this visibility, it becomes difficult to identify inefficiencies or explain technology costs to business stakeholders.

3. Manual budgeting and forecasting

Spreadsheet-based processes can make it challenging to maintain consistent assumptions, track changes, reconcile forecasts, and model different spending scenarios.

4. Complex cost allocation

Shared infrastructure, enterprise software, cybersecurity, networks, and support services often serve multiple business units.

Allocating these costs fairly requires reliable data, documented methodologies, and rules that finance and IT stakeholders understand.

5. Increasing expectations for financial accountability

CIOs and technology leaders are expected to explain not only what IT costs, but also how technology investments support business performance.

An ITFM tool can help connect financial data with operational consumption and business priorities.

These challenges create a business case for improving ITFM. However, they do not automatically mean that an organization needs to develop a custom application.

The next step is determining whether existing commercial capabilities can meet the requirements more effectively.

What Does It Mean to Build or Buy an ITFM Tool?

Before comparing the two approaches, it is important to distinguish them clearly.

Building a custom ITFM solution

Building means developing an internally owned application or set of components to support IT financial management.

The solution may use an organization's existing data warehouse, financial systems, business intelligence platform, APIs, and custom business logic.

A custom ITFM solution could include:

  • Data ingestion from financial and operational systems.
  • A centralized IT cost data model.
  • Custom allocation and cost attribution logic.
  • Budgeting and forecasting workflows.
  • Reporting dashboards.
  • Approval processes and financial controls.
  • Integration with existing enterprise applications.

Building does not necessarily mean creating every component from scratch. An organization can use existing databases, analytics tools, workflow engines, and cloud services while developing its own ITFM application layer.

Buying a commercial ITFM platform

Buying means licensing an established software product designed to support IT financial management or closely related requirements.

Depending on the product, commercial platforms may provide cost modeling, allocation rules, planning workflows, reporting, integrations, governance, and support for established ITFM or TBM practices.

Organizations generally configure the product around their financial processes rather than developing all capabilities themselves.

However, buying does not eliminate implementation work. Data integration, taxonomy mapping, allocation design, stakeholder alignment, testing, and ongoing administration remain important.

Choosing a hybrid approach

A hybrid strategy combines a commercial platform with custom-built components.

For example, an organization might purchase a platform for cost modeling and financial planning while building custom data pipelines, ERP integrations, business-specific allocation rules, and executive dashboards.

This can provide the advantages of a supported product without forcing every business requirement into a standard configuration.

What Are the Benefits of Building an ITFM Tool?

A custom solution can be valuable when an organization has distinctive requirements or already possesses a strong data and analytics foundation.

1. Greater control over business logic

Every organization has its own financial structures, allocation policies, reporting requirements, and operating models.

A custom solution allows the organization to implement rules around its specific business needs.

For example, a company may allocate technology costs using a combination of application usage, employee headcount, transaction volume, service consumption, and business-unit revenue.

A custom tool can implement these rules directly, provided the underlying data and governance are reliable.

2. Integration with existing technology

Organizations with mature data platforms may already have the infrastructure required to consolidate IT financial information.

A custom application can connect directly to existing ERP systems, general ledgers, procurement platforms, cloud billing data, CMDBs, and BI tools.

This may reduce duplication when the organization already has reliable integration capabilities.

However, custom integrations still require monitoring, error handling, data reconciliation, and maintenance as source systems change.

3. A tailored user experience

A custom ITFM solution can be designed around the way finance teams, technology leaders, service owners, and business stakeholders actually work.

For example, a CIO may need a strategic view of technology investment, while an infrastructure manager needs detailed consumption data and a finance business partner needs budget variance analysis.

A custom interface can prioritize these distinct workflows.

4. Potential long-term economic advantages

Building may be economically attractive when requirements are relatively narrow, the organization already has suitable technology, and the development effort is limited.

For example, an organization that needs only a few cost allocation models and executive dashboards may not require a full commercial ITFM platform.

However, potential savings should be evaluated against the cost of development, support, security, documentation, testing, and future enhancements.

5. Greater control over the product roadmap

An internally owned solution can evolve according to organizational priorities rather than a vendor's release schedule.

This can be useful when requirements change frequently or depend on proprietary operating models.

The trade-off is that internal teams must fund and deliver those changes.

What Are the Disadvantages of Building an ITFM Tool?

The initial development estimate rarely captures the full complexity of maintaining a financial management application.

1. Hidden development and maintenance costs

A custom ITFM tool requires more than a dashboard and a database.

The organization must consider:

  • Data engineering and integration.
  • Financial modeling and allocation logic.
  • User authentication and access controls.
  • Audit trails and reconciliation.
  • Automated testing and deployment.
  • Documentation and knowledge transfer.
  • Infrastructure, monitoring, and incident management.
  • Ongoing support and enhancements.

These responsibilities continue after the initial launch.

2. Longer time to business value

A custom application must be designed, developed, tested, and adopted.

Even when a prototype can be produced quickly, creating a production-ready solution with reliable financial controls may take substantially longer.

Meanwhile, business stakeholders continue to rely on existing processes.

3. Dependence on internal expertise

A custom tool may depend heavily on a small group of developers or analysts.

If these employees leave, the organization may lose important knowledge about allocation rules, data transformations, integrations, and reporting logic.

This creates operational risk unless the solution is properly documented and supported.

4. Difficulty maintaining financial accuracy

ITFM requires consistent definitions, traceable calculations, and reconciled financial information.

A custom solution must demonstrate that reported costs can be traced to their source, allocation methods are documented, and changes are controlled.

Financial errors can undermine confidence in the entire ITFM program.

5. Risk of recreating standard functionality

Building a custom application may require the organization to develop capabilities that commercial platforms already provide.

These include planning workflows, allocation engines, cost modeling, reporting structures, data connectors, and administrative controls.

If the organization does not have genuinely differentiated requirements, this effort may create limited strategic value.

Building is most attractive when the organization is solving a specific problem that existing tools cannot address economically—not simply because it wants to avoid software licensing fees.

What Are the Benefits of Buying an ITFM Tool?

A commercial platform can accelerate implementation when its capabilities align with the organization's requirements.

1. Access to established ITFM capabilities

Commercial ITFM platforms may provide functionality for cost transparency, cost allocation, planning, forecasting, reporting, and financial analysis.

This allows organizations to focus more of their effort on implementation, data quality, and business adoption instead of building foundational software components.

2. Faster implementation for standard requirements

If a product supports the required financial models and data sources, the organization can configure existing capabilities instead of developing equivalent functionality.

Actual implementation time depends on data complexity, integration requirements, procurement, and the organization's readiness.

3. Vendor support and product development

A commercial provider typically maintains the underlying product and releases updates, enhancements, and fixes.

This reduces the need for an internal team to own every aspect of the software lifecycle.

The organization must still manage configuration changes, platform administration, integration failures, and vendor relationships.

4. Access to established implementation practices

Experienced vendors and implementation partners may provide reference architectures, deployment methodologies, training, and guidance on ITFM or TBM practices.

These resources can help organizations avoid common implementation mistakes.

However, software alone does not create mature financial management processes. Organizations still need clear ownership, agreed allocation methodologies, and stakeholder participation.

5. A more predictable operating model

Subscription-based software can make some costs easier to forecast than a continuously evolving internal development program.

Nevertheless, total cost may increase as users, data volumes, modules, or service requirements expand.

Commercial terms should be reviewed carefully before committing to a platform.

What Are the Disadvantages of Buying an ITFM Tool?

Commercial software introduces a different set of risks.

1. Licensing and subscription costs

Commercial platforms may involve recurring license fees, implementation services, premium modules, training, and support costs.

Organizations should understand the full pricing model rather than comparing only the initial subscription quotation.

2. Limited customization

Some products support extensive configuration, while others constrain the financial models or workflows an organization can implement.

If business requirements are highly specialized, workarounds may become expensive or difficult to maintain.

3. Vendor lock-in

Financial models, reporting logic, integrations, and historical data may become tightly coupled to a particular platform.

Moving to another product can require data migration, model redesign, testing, retraining, and parallel operation.

A sound procurement process should address data ownership, export capabilities, API access, contract termination, and transition support.

4. Implementation complexity

Buying a product does not remove the need to integrate and reconcile data from different systems.

Poorly defined cost taxonomies, inconsistent financial periods, missing service mappings, and unreliable source data can delay implementation regardless of the software selected.

5. Dependence on the vendor roadmap

The vendor determines which core capabilities are developed and when they become available.

Organizations should verify that their essential requirements are supported today rather than assuming that planned features will arrive on schedule.

Build vs. Buy: How Much Does an ITFM Tool Cost?

The cost of ITFM software varies according to organizational scale, functionality, implementation complexity, data sources, and support requirements.

A useful comparison evaluates total cost of ownership (TCO) over three to five years.

Calculate the total cost of buying

A commercial ITFM platform's total cost may include:

  • Software licensing or subscription fees.
  • Implementation and configuration.
  • Data integration and migration.
  • Internal platform administration.
  • Training and change management.
  • Premium support and optional modules.
  • Future contract increases and exit costs.

A basic model is:

Buy TCO = Licensing + Implementation + Integration + Internal Operations + Training + Exit Costs

Calculate the total cost of building

A custom solution's total cost may include:

  • Initial development.
  • Architecture and data engineering.
  • Infrastructure and third-party software.
  • Security and compliance.
  • Testing and deployment.
  • Maintenance and support.
  • Future enhancements.
  • Documentation and staff replacement.

A basic model is:

Build TCO = Development + Infrastructure + Maintenance + Support + Enhancements + Transition Costs

Illustrative three-year cost comparison

Consider a hypothetical enterprise evaluating two alternatives. The following figures are examples only, not vendor quotations or industry averages.

Cost component Buy Build
Initial implementation or development $80,000 $250,000
Annual licensing or maintenance $120,000 $100,000
Annual internal administration or infrastructure $40,000 $20,000
Three-year total $560,000 $610,000

Under these assumptions, buying costs $50,000 less over three years.

However, the comparison changes when the licensing model, internal engineering requirements, or complexity of the custom application changes.

The example also excludes potential differences in time to value, capability, risk, and opportunity cost.

Why TCO alone is not enough

The lowest-cost option may not deliver the best business outcome.

An ITFM platform that improves forecast accuracy, identifies avoidable spending, reduces manual reconciliation, and increases accountability may create value beyond its direct operating cost.

Organizations should therefore evaluate both financial cost and business benefits.

Useful measures include:

  • Reduction in manual reporting effort.
  • Improvement in forecast accuracy.
  • Reduction in unreconciled IT expenditure.
  • Time required to produce cost reports.
  • Percentage of IT expenditure allocated to business services.
  • Savings identified and validated by finance.
  • Adoption by business-unit and service owners.

When Should You Build an ITFM Tool?

Building is worth considering when your requirements and existing capabilities support a focused development effort.

Situation Recommended approach
Basic IT cost dashboards using an existing data warehouse Build
Small organization with limited ITFM requirements Build or use existing financial tools
Unique allocation logic that cannot be configured economically Hybrid or targeted custom build
Complex enterprise-wide IT cost transparency Buy or hybrid
Mature budgeting, forecasting, and chargeback requirements Usually buy
Cloud cost optimization without broader ITFM requirements Evaluate specialized FinOps software
Strong engineering capabilities and a proprietary operating model Consider hybrid or build

The key is to distinguish a genuinely differentiated requirement from a standard capability that would be expensive to recreate.

For example, custom executive dashboards may be a sensible development investment. Rebuilding a complete financial planning engine, including permissions, audit trails, workflow management, and reconciliation, is a much larger undertaking.

When Should You Buy an ITFM Tool?

Buying is generally preferable when the organization needs a broad set of established capabilities and does not want to own the entire software development lifecycle.

Buying is particularly attractive when:

  1. You need enterprise-wide cost transparency and consistent financial reporting.
  2. You must implement cost allocation, showback, or chargeback across multiple business units.
  3. Your organization requires budgeting, forecasting, and scenario planning.
  4. You have numerous systems that must feed a common financial model.
  5. You need repeatable processes, vendor support, and a defined product roadmap.
  6. You want to accelerate adoption of established ITFM or TBM practices.

The most important condition is product fit.

A commercial platform is not necessarily the best choice if it cannot represent your core financial models, integrate with your source systems, or support your governance requirements without extensive customization.

Why a Hybrid ITFM Strategy Often Works Best

For many complex organizations, the real choice is not entirely build or entirely buy.

A hybrid approach separates standard financial management capabilities from business-specific requirements.

What should you buy?

Consider purchasing the capabilities that require a mature, supported foundation:

  • Core IT cost modeling.
  • Standard cost allocation functionality.
  • Budgeting and forecasting workflows.
  • Financial reporting and governance features.
  • Supported integrations and platform administration.

What should you build?

Consider developing components that provide a clear organizational advantage:

  • Custom data ingestion and transformation pipelines.
  • Proprietary service and application mappings.
  • Specialized allocation rules.
  • Business-specific workflow integrations.
  • Custom executive dashboards.
  • Advanced analytics tailored to your operating model.

The precise division depends on the selected product and its extensibility.

Example hybrid architecture

A typical architecture might look like this:

Source systems: ERP, general ledger, cloud billing, procurement, CMDB, and service management.

↓

Data integration layer: Data pipelines, validation, normalization, and reconciliation.

↓

ITFM platform: Cost modeling, allocations, financial planning, and reporting.

↓

Custom analytics and workflows: Business-specific dashboards, approvals, and specialized reporting.

↓

Business stakeholders: CIO, CFO, IT finance, service owners, and business-unit leaders.

This approach keeps the core financial management capability supported while preserving flexibility where it matters.

The architecture should also define a clear system of record for financial data, allocation logic, and reporting definitions to avoid duplicate calculations across platforms.

How to Choose the Right ITFM Strategy: A Step-by-Step Framework

A structured decision process helps prevent organizations from selecting technology before understanding the underlying business problem.

Step 1: Define the business objectives

Identify the decisions the ITFM solution must improve.

Examples include reducing technology expenditure, improving forecast accuracy, allocating costs fairly, or demonstrating the value of IT services.

Translate these objectives into measurable outcomes.

Step 2: Assess ITFM maturity

Evaluate current processes for cost visibility, financial planning, cost allocation, data governance, and stakeholder engagement.

An organization with fragmented spreadsheets and inconsistent definitions may need to standardize its financial processes before implementing sophisticated software.

Step 3: Document functional and technical requirements

Define essential capabilities and integration requirements.

Include data lineage, financial reconciliation, access controls, auditability, scalability, reporting, and exportability.

Separate must-have requirements from desirable enhancements.

Step 4: Evaluate your internal development capabilities

If considering a custom build, determine whether you have the necessary engineering capacity, product ownership, IT finance expertise, and long-term support model.

Account for staff availability and the opportunity cost of diverting resources from other priorities.

Step 5: Shortlist commercial products

Select vendors that can meet your essential requirements without excessive customization.

Ask vendors to demonstrate actual workflows using representative data rather than relying exclusively on standard demonstrations.

Step 6: Run a proof of concept

Test a small but representative use case, such as allocating infrastructure costs to business services and reconciling the result with finance.

Measure implementation effort, data quality requirements, reporting accuracy, usability, and ongoing administration.

Step 7: Compare risk-adjusted TCO

Compare the build, buy, and hybrid options over three to five years.

Include direct costs, internal labor, delivery risk, migration, technical debt, vendor dependency, and expected business benefits.

Step 8: Define a phased implementation roadmap

Start with the highest-value use case, validate the data model, establish ownership, and expand gradually.

A phased rollout helps build trust in financial reporting and reduces the risk of attempting enterprise-wide transformation before the underlying processes are ready.

Common Mistakes to Avoid When Choosing an ITFM Tool

Even a well-funded ITFM initiative can struggle if the decision focuses too narrowly on software.

Avoid these common mistakes:

Choosing based only on licensing cost. A lower subscription price may be offset by implementation effort, internal administration, or limited functionality.

Building before validating requirements. A custom prototype can become an expensive production application if financial controls and support requirements are discovered too late.

Assuming commercial software fixes data quality. Inconsistent source data and unclear allocation rules remain organizational problems.

Over-customizing a commercial platform. Excessive customization can complicate upgrades, increase support costs, and reduce the benefits of purchasing a standard product.

Ignoring business adoption. Cost reports create value only when stakeholders understand them and use them to make decisions.

Failing to plan for vendor exit or internal handover. Data portability, documentation, and ownership should be addressed from the beginning.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it cheaper to build or buy an ITFM tool?

Neither option is always cheaper. Building can be economical for narrow requirements when an organization already has suitable data infrastructure and engineering capacity. Buying can be more economical when many established ITFM capabilities would otherwise need to be developed and maintained.

The most reliable comparison is a three- to five-year TCO analysis.

How long does it take to implement an ITFM tool?

Implementation time depends on data readiness, integrations, the number of services and business units, the complexity of allocation rules, and organizational change requirements.

A focused proof of concept may be delivered much sooner than a complete enterprise rollout. Neither a custom build nor a commercial implementation should be assigned a fixed timeline without a defined scope.

Can Power BI be used as an ITFM tool?

Power BI can support IT financial reporting, analytics, and dashboards. Combined with a data warehouse and carefully designed financial models, it can also support some cost allocation and showback use cases.

However, Power BI alone is not a complete ITFM operating model. Complex financial planning, workflow management, governed allocations, auditability, and integrations may require additional systems or custom development.

What is the difference between ITFM and FinOps?

ITFM covers financial management across the broader IT estate, including infrastructure, software, personnel, shared services, budgeting, allocation, and financial reporting.

FinOps focuses on financial accountability and the value derived from cloud and technology consumption, with an emphasis on collaboration between engineering, finance, and business teams.

The disciplines can complement one another, but a FinOps tool may not meet all enterprise ITFM requirements.

What is the difference between ITFM and TBM?

ITFM focuses on managing and understanding IT financial performance. Technology Business Management provides a broader framework for connecting technology costs and consumption with the services, capabilities, and business outcomes they support.

The terms overlap in practice, and many ITFM platforms support TBM-oriented processes.

Should a company build a custom ITFM tool if it already has a data warehouse?

Not necessarily. An existing data warehouse reduces some integration and infrastructure requirements, but it does not automatically provide financial planning workflows, allocation governance, audit trails, or a maintainable application.

A custom solution makes sense when the required capabilities are limited or highly differentiated and the organization can support them over time.

Can an organization start with a custom solution and buy later?

Yes. This can be a sensible transition strategy if the initial solution addresses a narrow problem and the organization defines clear migration requirements.

To preserve future options, maintain documented data models, transparent allocation logic, portable data, and clear separation between data pipelines and presentation layers.

What is the biggest risk of building an ITFM tool?

The biggest risk is underestimating the ongoing work required to maintain accurate financial models, integrations, controls, and business rules.

A solution that initially appears inexpensive can become costly if it depends on a small number of employees or cannot adapt to changing systems and financial requirements.

Conclusion: Should You Build or Buy an ITFM Tool?

The decision to build or buy an IT Financial Management tool should be driven by business requirements, internal capabilities, financial management maturity, and long-term total cost of ownership.

Buy when you need comprehensive, established ITFM capabilities. Build when your requirements are narrow and differentiated. Choose hybrid when you need a mature financial management foundation with the flexibility to support your unique business model.

For most large organizations, a hybrid strategy offers a compelling balance: use commercial software for core financial management and develop custom components only where they provide measurable value.

Ultimately, the goal is not to own the software or minimize the license fee. It is to create reliable cost transparency, improve financial decisions, increase accountability, and ensure that technology spending supports business outcomes.

Start by defining the decisions you want to improve, assessing your data readiness, and comparing realistic three- to five-year costs. From there, select the approach that provides the strongest combination of business value, operational reliability, and long-term flexibility.
